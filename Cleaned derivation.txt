lets rederive the equations of the entire problem from scratch and walk through the theory of this problem

Yes. The cleanest way to understand the project is to derive it as a **coupled flight-dynamics + energy + mass-closure problem**.

The central idea is:

$$
\boxed{\text{design determines dynamics} \quad\leftrightarrow\quad
\text{dynamics determine energy} \quad\leftrightarrow\quad
\text{energy determines design}}
$$

So there are really two mathematical layers:

$$
\boxed{\text{Sizing/algebraic equations}}
$$

and

$$
\boxed{\text{Physical differential equations in time}}
$$

They couple through mass, inertia, rotor area, battery capacity, motor capability, and the mission.

---

# 1. Define the machine

Suppose we have \(N\) rotors.

Let

$$
D=\text{rotor diameter}
$$

so one rotor has disk area

$$
A_r=\frac{\pi D^2}{4}.
$$

Total actuator disk area:

$$
\boxed{
A=N\frac{\pi D^2}{4}
}
$$

Now divide the mass into:

$$
\boxed{
m=m_{\rm fix}+m_{\rm str}+m_{\rm prop}+m_{\rm bat}
}
$$

where:

* \(m_{\rm fix}\): screen, computer, tracking camera, electronics, gimbal, etc.
* \(m_{\rm str}\): frame and structural hardware
* \(m_{\rm prop}\): motors, ESCs, propellers
* \(m_{\rm bat}\): battery

This is our first equation.

But the masses on the right are not independent.

That's where the coupling starts.

---

# 2. First physical requirement: it must support its weight

Weight is

$$
W=mg.
$$

In perfect hover,

$$
\boxed{T=mg}
$$

where \(T\) is total rotor thrust.

If all \(N\) motors share thrust equally:

$$
T_i=\frac{mg}{N}.
$$

But motors cannot be designed such that hover is 100% throttle.

Introduce a peak-thrust ratio

$$
\lambda>1.
$$

Then

$$
\boxed{
T_{\max}=\lambda mg.
}
$$

For example,

$$
\lambda=2
$$

means the vehicle has maximum thrust equal to twice its weight.

This is important for acceleration, control authority, wind rejection, etc.

---

# 3. Motor mass coupling

Suppose the propulsion hardware has specific thrust

$$
S_m
=
\frac{\text{maximum thrust}}
{\text{propulsion mass}}
$$

with units

$$
[S_m]=\frac{N}{kg}.
$$

Then

$$
S_m=\frac{T_{\max}}{m_{\rm prop}}
$$

so

$$
m_{\rm prop}
=
\frac{T_{\max}}{S_m}.
$$

Since

$$
T_{\max}=\lambda mg,
$$

we obtain

$$
\boxed{
m_{\rm prop}
=
\frac{\lambda g}{S_m}m.
}
$$

Define

$$
\gamma_m=
\frac{\lambda g}{S_m}.
$$

Then

$$
\boxed{m_{\rm prop}=\gamma_m m.}
$$

So motor mass scales **linearly** with aircraft mass in this simplified model.

This feedback is therefore not inherently catastrophic.

---

# 4. Structure coupling

For a first model, represent structural mass as a fraction of gross mass:

$$
\boxed{
m_{\rm str}=f_s m.
}
$$

For example,

$$
f_s=0.20
$$

means the structural system is 20% of gross mass.

This isn't a fundamental law.

A more detailed model would derive structural mass from bending, buckling and stiffness:

$$
\sigma=\frac{Mc}{I},
$$

with rotor-arm bending moment roughly

$$
M\sim T_i L.
$$

Eventually we should replace \(f_s\) with an actual beam model.

But \(f_s m\) is good for seeing the global coupling.

---

# 5. Now derive hover power from momentum theory

This is where the important nonlinear term appears.

Imagine the rotor system as an actuator disk.

In hover, air approaches approximately stationary.

The rotor accelerates a mass flow downward.

Let the induced velocity through the disk be

$$
v_i.
$$

Momentum theory gives the far-wake velocity:

$$
v_{\rm wake}=2v_i.
$$

Mass flow through the disk:

$$
\dot m_{\rm air}=\rho A v_i.
$$

Thrust is rate of momentum change:

$$
T=\dot m_{\rm air}(2v_i).
$$

Therefore

$$
T=2\rho A v_i^2.
$$

Solve for induced velocity:

$$
\boxed{
v_i=
\sqrt{\frac{T}{2\rho A}}
}
$$

and since hover requires

$$
T=mg,
$$

$$
\boxed{
v_i=
\sqrt{\frac{mg}{2\rho A}}.
}
$$

---

# 6. Ideal hover power

Power is force times velocity:

$$
P_i=T v_i.
$$

Therefore

$$
P_i
=
T\sqrt{\frac{T}{2\rho A}}.
$$

Thus

$$
\boxed{
P_i=
\frac{T^{3/2}}{\sqrt{2\rho A}}
}
$$

and in hover:

$$
\boxed{
P_i=
\frac{(mg)^{3/2}}
{\sqrt{2\rho A}}.
}
$$

Here is the first major nonlinear law:

$$
\boxed{
P_i\propto m^{3/2}
}
$$

**if rotor area is fixed.**

---

# 7. Real power

Real rotors aren't ideal.

Introduce rotor figure of merit

$$
FM<1
$$

and electrical efficiency

$$
\eta<1.
$$

Then approximately

$$
P_{\rm shaft}
=
\frac{P_i}{FM}
$$

and

$$
P_{\rm elec}
=
\frac{P_{\rm shaft}}{\eta}.
$$

Therefore

$$
\boxed{
P_{\rm prop}
=
\frac{(mg)^{3/2}}
{FM\,\eta\,\sqrt{2\rho A}}
}
$$

for steady hover.

There is also screen/computer power:

$$
P_{\rm aux}
=
P_{\rm screen}
+
P_{\rm computer}
+
P_{\rm sensors}.
$$

Total electrical power:

$$
\boxed{
P_{\rm total}
=
\frac{(mg)^{3/2}}
{FM\,\eta\,\sqrt{2\rho A}}
+
P_{\rm aux}.
}
$$

---

# 8. Battery mass

Let usable battery specific energy be

$$
e_b
$$

in J/kg.

For reference,

$$
250\,Wh/kg
=
250(3600)
=
900\,000\,J/kg.
$$

For hover duration \(t_f\),

$$
E=P_{\rm total}t_f.
$$

Battery mass therefore is

$$
m_{\rm bat}
=
\frac{E}{e_b}.
$$

Hence

$$
\boxed{
m_{\rm bat}
=
\frac{t_f}{e_b}
\left[
\frac{(mg)^{3/2}}
{FM\,\eta\sqrt{2\rho A}}
+
P_{\rm aux}
\right].
}
$$

Now we have the vicious loop:

$$
m
\rightarrow P
\rightarrow m_{\rm bat}
\rightarrow m.
$$

---

# 9. Put everything into the mass equation

Start with

$$
m=
m_{\rm fix}
+m_{\rm str}
+m_{\rm prop}
+m_{\rm bat}.
$$

Substitute:

$$
m
=
m_{\rm fix}
+
f_sm
+
\frac{\lambda g}{S_m}m
+
\frac{t_f}{e_b}
\left[
\frac{(mg)^{3/2}}
{FM\,\eta\sqrt{2\rho A}}
+
P_{\rm aux}
\right].
$$

Define

$$
\alpha
=
1-f_s-\frac{\lambda g}{S_m}
$$

and

$$
\beta
=
\frac{
t_f g^{3/2}
}{
e_b FM\eta\sqrt{2\rho A}
}
$$

and define the effective fixed load

$$
M_0
=
m_{\rm fix}
+
\frac{P_{\rm aux}t_f}{e_b}.
$$

Then the entire first-order sizing problem collapses to

$$
\boxed{
\alpha m-\beta m^{3/2}=M_0.
}
$$

This is one of the most important equations in the project.

---

# 10. Why this equation creates a feasibility wall

Define

$$
F(m)=\alpha m-\beta m^{3/2}.
$$

At small \(m\), the linear term wins.

But eventually

$$
m^{3/2}
$$

grows faster than \(m\).

So \(F(m)\) rises, reaches a maximum, then falls.

Differentiate:

$$
F'(m)
=
\alpha
-
\frac32\beta\sqrt m.
$$

Critical point:

$$
\alpha
=
\frac32\beta\sqrt{m^*}.
$$

Therefore

$$
\sqrt{m^*}
=
\frac{2\alpha}{3\beta}
$$

and

$$
\boxed{
m^*
=
\frac{4\alpha^2}{9\beta^2}.
}
$$

Now substitute this into \(F(m)\):

$$
\boxed{
M_{0,\max}
=
\frac{4\alpha^3}{27\beta^2}.
}
$$

So:

$$
\boxed{
M_0>M_{0,\max}
\quad\Rightarrow\quad
\text{no physical mass solution}.
}
$$

Not just inconvenient.

Not just very heavy.

Under the model:

$$
\boxed{\text{no equilibrium design exists}.}
$$

---

# 11. Why endurance is so brutal

Because

$$
\beta\propto t_f
$$

and

$$
M_{0,\max}\propto\frac{1}{\beta^2},
$$

we obtain

$$
\boxed{
M_{0,\max}\propto\frac1{t_f^2}.
}
$$

More explicitly,

$$
\boxed{
M_{0,\max}
=
\frac{
8\alpha^3
\rho A
e_b^2
FM^2
\eta^2
}{
27g^3t_f^2
}.
}
$$

Therefore:

$$
M_{\max}\propto A,
$$

$$
M_{\max}\propto e_b^2,
$$

$$
M_{\max}\propto FM^2,
$$

$$
M_{\max}\propto\eta^2,
$$

and

$$
\boxed{
M_{\max}\propto t_f^{-2}.
}
$$

That is the mathematical reason long hover endurance becomes difficult.

---

# 12. But the \(m^{3/2}\) law contains an assumption

We assumed

$$
A=\text{constant}.
$$

Look again:

$$
P
\propto
\frac{m^{3/2}}{\sqrt A}.
$$

Suppose rotor area itself scales as

$$
A\propto m^p.
$$

Then

$$
\sqrt A\propto m^{p/2}.
$$

Therefore

$$
\boxed{
P\propto
m^{(3-p)/2}.
}
$$

This gives a beautiful general result.

### Fixed rotor area

$$
p=0
$$

therefore

$$
P\propto m^{3/2}.
$$

Nonlinear battery runaway.

### Constant disk loading

If

$$
A\propto m,
$$

then

$$
p=1
$$

and

$$
\boxed{P\propto m.}
$$

Battery mass becomes linear.

### Rotor area grows faster than mass

If

$$
p>1,
$$

then

$$
P\propto m^q,
\qquad q<1.
$$

No asymptotic battery runaway.

But now the physical machine becomes enormous.

So the problem hasn't vanished; the limiting constraint has shifted from energy to geometry/structure/safety.

---

# 13. Disk loading reveals this immediately

Define disk loading

$$
DL=\frac{mg}{A}.
$$

Then

$$
v_i=
\sqrt{\frac{DL}{2\rho}}.
$$

Hover power per unit mass is

$$
\frac{P_i}{m}
=
g\sqrt{\frac{DL}{2\rho}}.
$$

Including losses:

$$
\boxed{
\frac{P_{\rm prop}}{m}
=
\frac{g}{FM\eta}
\sqrt{\frac{DL}{2\rho}}.
}
$$

This equation tells you something fundamental:

> A hovering machine isn't mainly asking for "powerful motors". It is asking for **low enough disk loading**.

Huge rotors → low disk loading → lower induced velocity → lower power.

---

# 14. Constant-disk-loading mass closure

If \(DL\) stays constant,

$$
P_{\rm prop}=c_Pm
$$

where

$$
c_P=
\frac{g}{FM\eta}
\sqrt{\frac{DL}{2\rho}}.
$$

Battery mass becomes

$$
m_{\rm bat}
=
\frac{c_Pt_f}{e_b}m.
$$

Define battery mass fraction

$$
\gamma_b
=
\frac{c_Pt_f}{e_b}.
$$

Then

$$
m
=
M_0
+
f_sm
+
\gamma_m m
+
\gamma_bm.
$$

Therefore

$$
\boxed{
m=
\frac{M_0}
{
1-f_s-\gamma_m-\gamma_b
}.
}
$$

Now there is no \(m^{3/2}\) fold.

Instead feasibility requires simply

$$
\boxed{
f_s+\gamma_m+\gamma_b<1.
}
$$

As the denominator approaches zero,

$$
m\rightarrow\infty.
$$

So there is still an endurance boundary, but its mathematical nature changes.

---

# 15. Until now we have not written the flight differential equations

Everything above is mostly a **design closure problem**.

Once the machine exists, its actual mass is essentially constant:

$$
\boxed{\dot m=0.}
$$

The battery loses energy, not meaningful physical mass.

Now we describe flight.

Let position in inertial coordinates be

$$
\mathbf r=
\begin{bmatrix}
x\\y\\z
\end{bmatrix}.
$$

Velocity:

$$
\mathbf v=\dot{\mathbf r}.
$$

First equation:

$$
\boxed{
\dot{\mathbf r}=\mathbf v.
}
$$

Newton's second law:

$$
m\dot{\mathbf v}
=
\sum\mathbf F.
$$

For the aircraft:

$$
\boxed{
m\dot{\mathbf v}
=
T R\mathbf e_3
-
mg\mathbf e_3
+
\mathbf F_D
+
\mathbf F_{\rm disturbance}.
}
$$

Here:

* \(R\) = body-to-world rotation matrix
* \(T\) = total thrust
* \(\mathbf F_D\) = aerodynamic drag

---

# 16. Screen drag

The screen is unfortunately a large flat aerodynamic surface.

A simple drag model:

$$
\boxed{
\mathbf F_D
=
-\frac12
\rho C_D A_{\rm proj}
|\mathbf v_{\rm rel}|
\mathbf v_{\rm rel}.
}
$$

Relative air velocity:

$$
\mathbf v_{\rm rel}
=
\mathbf v-\mathbf v_{\rm wind}.
$$

For a flat screen,

$$
A_{\rm proj}
$$

changes with orientation.

This means the screen itself couples attitude to translational dynamics.

---

# 17. Rotational dynamics

Define body angular velocity

$$
\boldsymbol\omega=
\begin{bmatrix}
p\\q\\r
\end{bmatrix}.
$$

Rigid-body Euler equation:

$$
\boxed{
J\dot{\boldsymbol\omega}
+
\boldsymbol\omega
\times
J\boldsymbol\omega
=
\boldsymbol\tau.
}
$$

Thus

$$
\boxed{
\dot{\boldsymbol\omega}
=
J^{-1}
\left[
\boldsymbol\tau
-
\boldsymbol\omega\times J\boldsymbol\omega
\right].
}
$$

Attitude evolution can be written

$$
\boxed{
\dot R=R[\boldsymbol\omega]_\times
}
$$

or using a quaternion

$$
\boxed{
\dot q=
\frac12\Omega(\boldsymbol\omega)q.
}
$$

---

# 18. Rotor equations

For rotor \(i\),

$$
\boxed{
T_i=k_T\Omega_i^2.
}
$$

Reaction torque:

$$
\boxed{
Q_i=k_Q\Omega_i^2.
}
$$

Total thrust:

$$
\boxed{
T=\sum_{i=1}^{N}k_T\Omega_i^2.
}
$$

Moment from rotor \(i\):

$$
\boldsymbol\tau_i
=
\mathbf r_i
\times
T_i\mathbf e_3
+
s_iQ_i\mathbf e_3.
$$

Therefore

$$
\boxed{
\boldsymbol\tau
=
\sum_i\boldsymbol\tau_i.
}
$$

So motor speed controls both translation and orientation.

---

# 19. Motor dynamics are themselves differential equations

Motors don't instantaneously jump to commanded RPM.

A simple model is

$$
\boxed{
\tau_m\dot\Omega_i
=
\Omega_{i,\rm cmd}-\Omega_i.
}
$$

A more physical DC/BLDC approximation is:

Electrical:

$$
\boxed{
L\dot i_i
=
V_i-R_mi_i-k_e\Omega_i
}
$$

Mechanical:

$$
\boxed{
J_r\dot\Omega_i
=
k_\tau i_i-Q_{\rm prop}.
}
$$

With

$$
Q_{\rm prop}\approx k_Q\Omega_i^2.
$$

Now we already have many coupled ODEs.

---

# 20. Battery dynamics

Battery energy really is a time state.

Let

$$
E(t)
$$

be remaining usable energy.

Then

$$
\boxed{
\dot E=-P_{\rm total}.
}
$$

Approximately,

$$
P_{\rm rotor,i}\propto\Omega_i^3.
$$

So

$$
\boxed{
\dot E
=
-
\left[
\sum_i k_P\Omega_i^3
+
P_{\rm aux}
\right].
}
$$

Or using state of charge

$$
s=\frac{E}{E_{\max}},
$$

we have

$$
\boxed{
\dot s
=
-\frac{P_{\rm total}}{E_{\max}}.
}
$$

This is a **real differential coupling**.

Aggressive control:

$$
\Omega_i\uparrow
$$

causes

$$
P\uparrow
$$

causes

$$
SOC\downarrow.
$$

---

# 21. Human-following dynamics

Suppose your position is measured as

$$
\mathbf r_h(t).
$$

We don't want the screen at your position.

We want some offset:

$$
\mathbf d.
$$

Desired screen position:

$$
\boxed{
\mathbf r_d(t)=\mathbf r_h(t)+\mathbf d(t).
}
$$

Tracking error:

$$
\boxed{
\mathbf e_p=\mathbf r_d-\mathbf r.
}
$$

Velocity error:

$$
\mathbf e_v=
\dot{\mathbf r}_d-\mathbf v.
$$

A simple outer-loop controller might request acceleration

$$
\boxed{
\mathbf a_c=
\ddot{\mathbf r}_d
+
K_p\mathbf e_p
+
K_d\mathbf e_v.
}
$$

Required force becomes

$$
\boxed{
\mathbf F_c
=
m(\mathbf a_c+g\mathbf e_3).
}
$$

Then desired thrust:

$$
\boxed{
T_c=\|\mathbf F_c\|.
}
$$

And desired vehicle orientation is chosen so the thrust axis aligns with \(\mathbf F_c\).

This is where control theory enters.

---

# 22. Near-hover linearization

For small roll \(\phi\) and pitch \(\theta\), the nonlinear equations simplify.

Approximately,

$$
\boxed{
\ddot x\approx g\theta
}
$$

$$
\boxed{
\ddot y\approx-g\phi
}
$$

$$
\boxed{
\ddot z\approx\frac{\delta T}{m}
}
$$

and

$$
\boxed{
\ddot\phi=\frac{\tau_x}{J_x}
}
$$

$$
\boxed{
\ddot\theta=\frac{\tau_y}{J_y}
}
$$

$$
\boxed{
\ddot\psi=\frac{\tau_z}{J_z}.
}
$$

Notice something interesting:

To move sideways,

$$
\theta
$$

must change first.

So the chain is

$$
\tau_y
\rightarrow
\ddot\theta
\rightarrow
\theta
\rightarrow
\ddot x
\rightarrow
x.
$$

That's why multirotors normally use cascaded control loops:

$$
\boxed{
\text{position}
\rightarrow
\text{velocity}
\rightarrow
\text{attitude}
\rightarrow
\text{angular rate}
\rightarrow
\text{motor speed}.
}
$$

---

# 23. Screen stabilization can be another dynamic system

If the screen sits on a gimbal,

$$
J_g\ddot\theta_g
=
\tau_g
-\tau_{\rm wind}
-b_g\dot\theta_g.
$$

Therefore

$$
\boxed{
J_g\ddot\theta_g
+b_g\dot\theta_g
=
\tau_g-\tau_{\rm wind}.
}
$$

You can control

$$
\theta_g
$$

independently of aircraft attitude.

That's useful because the vehicle may tilt 10–20° to accelerate while you still want the screen vertical.

---

# 24. Now we can write the complete dynamic state

One possible state vector is

$$
\boxed{
x=
\begin{bmatrix}
\mathbf r\\
\mathbf v\\
q\\
\boldsymbol\omega\\
\Omega_1\\
\vdots\\
\Omega_N\\
E\\
\theta_g\\
\dot\theta_g
\end{bmatrix}.
}
$$

Then conceptually,

$$
\boxed{
\dot x=f(x,u,w;p)
}
$$

where:

* \(u\): motor/gimbal commands
* \(w\): disturbances such as wind and human motion
* \(p\): physical design parameters

with

$$
p=
\{
m,J,A,D,N,E_{\max},k_T,k_Q,\ldots
\}.
$$

---

# 25. But those design parameters are themselves coupled

This closes the circle.

Battery capacity:

$$
E_{\max}=e_bm_{\rm bat}.
$$

Motor capability determines

$$
T_{\max}.
$$

Rotor geometry determines

$$
A.
$$

All those determine dynamics.

And the dynamics determine required energy.

For an arbitrary mission, the battery equation shouldn't even be

$$
E=Pt.
$$

The correct expression is

$$
\boxed{
E_{\rm mission}
=
\int_0^{t_f}
P(x(t),u(t),p)\,dt.
}
$$

Therefore

$$
\boxed{
m_{\rm bat}
=
\frac{1}{e_b}
\int_0^{t_f}
P(x,u,p)\,dt.
}
$$

This is the most general battery-mass equation.

---

# 26. The true mass-closure equation

We can now write

$$
\boxed{
m=
m_{\rm fix}
+
m_{\rm str}(p,\text{loads})
+
m_{\rm prop}(T_{\max})
+
\frac1{e_b}
\int_0^{t_f}
P(x,u,p)\,dt.
}
$$

And the trajectory must satisfy

$$
\boxed{
\dot x=f(x,u;p).
}
$$

That is really the mathematical heart of your flying-screen problem.

The battery mass depends on the trajectory.

The trajectory depends on the controller.

The controller's effectiveness depends on mass and inertia.

Mass and inertia depend partly on the battery.

So:

$$
\boxed{
m
\rightarrow
J
\rightarrow
\text{dynamics}
\rightarrow
u(t)
\rightarrow
P(t)
\rightarrow
E
\rightarrow
m_{\rm battery}
\rightarrow
m.
}
$$

That's the true coupling.

---

# 27. The full problem is therefore a co-design problem

You are not merely designing an aircraft.

You're choosing both physical parameters

$$
p
$$

and control law

$$
u(t).
$$

You might formulate:

$$
\boxed{
\min_{p,u(t)}
J
}
$$

where, for example,

$$
J=
w_m m
+
w_EE_{\rm mission}
+
w_ee_{\rm tracking}^2
+
w_NN_{\rm noise}.
$$

Subject to:

$$
\dot x=f(x,u;p)
$$

and

$$
m=
m_{\rm fix}
+m_{\rm str}
+m_{\rm prop}
+m_{\rm bat},
$$

plus constraints such as

$$
T(t)\le T_{\max},
$$

$$
SOC(t)\ge SOC_{\min},
$$

$$
|\Omega_i|\le\Omega_{\max},
$$

$$
d_{\rm human}\ge d_{\rm safe},
$$

$$
|\theta_{\rm screen}|
\le\theta_{\rm readable},
$$

and

$$
t_f\ge t_{\rm desired}.
$$

This is essentially a **multidisciplinary design optimization / control co-design problem**.

---

# 28. The theoretical hierarchy of the project

I'd think of it as seven layers:

$$
\boxed{
\textbf{1. Momentum theory}
}
$$

determines hover energy.

↓

$$
\boxed{
\textbf{2. Mass closure}
}
$$

determines whether the design can physically exist.

↓

$$
\boxed{
\textbf{3. Structural mechanics}
}
$$

determines frame mass and stiffness.

↓

$$
\boxed{
\textbf{4. Newton–Euler dynamics}
}
$$

determines how the flying body moves.

↓

$$
\boxed{
\textbf{5. Motor + battery dynamics}
}
$$

determine actuator response and energy use.

↓

$$
\boxed{
\textbf{6. Control theory}
}
$$

keeps it stable and follows you.

↓

$$
\boxed{
\textbf{7. Optimization}
}
$$

chooses rotor size, battery, motor, geometry, and controller jointly.

---

## And the single most important equation at each level is

**Lift**

$$
T=mg.
$$

**Hover power**

$$
\boxed{
P=
\frac{(mg)^{3/2}}
{FM\eta\sqrt{2\rho A}}
}
$$

**Mass closure**

$$
\boxed{
m=m_{\rm fix}+m_{\rm str}+m_{\rm prop}+m_{\rm bat}.
}
$$

**Battery**

$$
\boxed{
m_{\rm bat}
=
\frac1{e_b}
\int P\,dt.
}
$$

**Translation**

$$
\boxed{
m\dot v=\sum F.
}
$$

**Rotation**

$$
\boxed{
J\dot\omega+\omega\times J\omega=\tau.
}
$$

**Control**

$$
\boxed{
u=\mathcal K(x_d-x).
}
$$

And the whole project becomes:

$$
\boxed{
\begin{cases}
\dot x=f(x,u,p)\\[1mm]
0=h(x,u,p,m)\\[1mm]
m=m_{\rm fix}+m_{\rm str}+m_{\rm prop}
+\dfrac1{e_b}\displaystyle\int_0^{t_f}P\,dt
\end{cases}
}
$$

That is the formulation I would use as the mathematical foundation for the flying-screen project.

The especially interesting next step is to stop assuming \(m_{\rm str}=f_sm\) and \(S_m=\) constant, and derive **rotor diameter, motor torque/RPM, arm bending, propeller tip speed and structural mass from geometry**. At that point we would have a much closer-to-first-principles model rather than a drone-sizing approximation.

I think we can write code to solve all this

Yes. Once we have the physics in equation form, this becomes a **computational engineering problem**. And code lets us go much further than the closed-form equations because we no longer need assumptions like constant hover power, fixed drag, or constant motor efficiency.

The software can answer a question like:

$$
\boxed{
\text{Given a screen, mission and available technology, does any feasible aircraft exist?}
}
$$

and, if yes,

$$
\boxed{
\text{What rotor size, battery, motors, geometry and controller minimize mass/power/noise?}
}
$$

**The key computational structure** is that we have three things at once:

$$
\boxed{\dot x=f(x,u,p)}
$$

for the flight dynamics,

$$
\boxed{0=g(x,u,p)}
$$

for algebraic/design constraints, and

$$
\boxed{\min_p J(p)}
$$

for selecting the best design.

Here \(x\) contains the changing physical state,

$$
x=
[
r,\,
v,\,
q,\,
\omega,\,
\Omega_1\dots\Omega_N,\,
E,\,
\theta_g,\,
\dot\theta_g
],
$$

while \(p\) contains the design itself,

$$
p=
[
D,\,
N,\,
m_b,\,
L_{\rm arm},\,
S_m,\,
e_b,\,
k_T,\,
k_Q,\,
J,\ldots
].
$$

The code would naturally evolve through roughly these stages:

1. **Static feasibility solver.** Solve the mass closure and reproduce our analytical result

   $$
   \alpha m-\beta m^{3/2}=M_0.
   $$

   This becomes our sanity check: numerical answers must match the closed-form theory.

2. **First-principles component sizing.** Replace crude parameters such as \(f_s\) and \(S_m\) with rotor geometry, motor torque/RPM, arm stress, battery characteristics, and actual masses.

3. **6-DOF nonlinear simulation.** Integrate

   $$
   m\dot v=\sum F,
   \qquad
   J\dot\omega+\omega\times J\omega=\tau
   $$

   together with motor and battery dynamics.

4. **Controller.** Put the human trajectory \(r_h(t)\) into the simulation and implement the cascaded position/attitude controller.

5. **Mission-energy calculation.** Instead of assuming \(E=Pt\), compute

   $$
   \boxed{
   E_{\rm mission}=
   \int_0^{t_f}P(x(t),u(t))\,dt.
   }
   $$

6. **Close the battery loop.** Required battery mass becomes

   $$
   m_b=\frac{E_{\rm mission}}{e_b},
   $$

   but changing \(m_b\) changes aircraft mass, which changes the simulation, which changes \(E_{\rm mission}\). The solver iterates until this converges.

7. **Optimization.** Search rotor diameter, rotor number, battery size, motor size, frame geometry and perhaps controller parameters for the best feasible design.

The interesting part is stage 6. Imagine that we guess

$$
m_b=1.0\ {\rm kg}.
$$

The program computes total mass:

$$
m=3.4\ {\rm kg}.
$$

It simulates your one-hour work mission and finds

$$
E_{\rm mission}=1.4\ {\rm MJ}.
$$

If usable battery energy density is

$$
e_b=900\,000\ {\rm J/kg},
$$

then the battery actually required is

$$
m_{b,\rm req}
=
\frac{1.4\times10^6}{9\times10^5}
=
1.56\ {\rm kg}.
$$

So our original battery was too small. The program tries \(1.56\) kg. But now the aircraft is heavier, so energy consumption increases, perhaps requiring \(1.85\) kg. Then:

$$
1.0
\rightarrow1.56
\rightarrow1.85
\rightarrow2.02
\rightarrow2.11
\rightarrow2.15
\rightarrow\cdots
$$

If it converges,

$$
\boxed{m_b\rightarrow m_b^*}
$$

we have a feasible design.

If instead

$$
1
\rightarrow1.6
\rightarrow2.4
\rightarrow4
\rightarrow8
\rightarrow20\rightarrow\cdots
$$

we have computationally discovered the runaway condition.

More rigorously, we wouldn't simply iterate. We'd define residual equations.

For the simplest model, let the unknowns be

$$
y=
\begin{bmatrix}
m\\
m_b
\end{bmatrix}.
$$

Define mass residual:

$$
R_1=
m-
\left(
m_{\rm fix}
+m_{\rm str}(m)
+m_{\rm prop}(m)
+m_b
\right).
$$

Then simulate the mission and calculate \(E_{\rm mission}\). The energy residual is

$$
R_2=
e_bm_b-E_{\rm mission}(m,p,u).
$$

The true aircraft satisfies

$$
\boxed{
R_1=0,\qquad R_2=0.
}
$$

So computationally we're asking a nonlinear solver for

$$
\boxed{
R(y)=
\begin{bmatrix}
R_1\\R_2
\end{bmatrix}
=
0.
}
$$

A simplified Python architecture would conceptually look like:

```python
def design_residual(y, design, mission):
    mass, battery_mass = y

    structure_mass = structure_model(mass, design)
    propulsion_mass = propulsion_model(mass, design)

    mass_residual = (
        mass
        - design.fixed_mass
        - structure_mass
        - propulsion_mass
        - battery_mass
    )

    result = simulate_flight(
        mass=mass,
        battery_mass=battery_mass,
        design=design,
        mission=mission,
    )

    energy_required = result.energy_used
    energy_available = battery_mass * design.usable_specific_energy

    energy_residual = energy_available - energy_required

    return [
        mass_residual,
        energy_residual,
    ]
```

Then numerically:

```python
solution = root(
    design_residual,
    x0=[3.0, 1.0],
    args=(design, mission),
)
```

But `simulate_flight()` is where the interesting engineering lives. Inside it we'd numerically integrate something like

$$
\dot r=v,
$$

$$
\dot v=
\frac{1}{m}
\left(
TR(q)e_3
-mge_3
+F_D
\right),
$$

$$
\dot q=
\frac12\Omega(\omega)q,
$$

$$
\dot\omega=
J^{-1}
[
\tau-\omega\times J\omega
],
$$

$$
\dot\Omega_i=
\frac{\Omega_{i,c}-\Omega_i}{\tau_m},
$$

$$
\boxed{
\dot E=-P(x,u).
}
$$

Numerical integration gives

$$
x(t_0)\rightarrow x(t_f).
$$

For Python, something like `solve_ivp` is perfectly suitable for the first nonlinear simulator.

Then we get to an even more interesting computational formulation. Suppose our design vector is

$$
p=
[
D,N,m_b,L_{\rm arm},\ldots].
$$

We might minimize

$$
\boxed{
J(p)
=
w_m m
+w_P\bar P
+w_NL_{\rm noise}
+w_se_{\rm tracking}
}
$$

subject to

$$
T_{\max}\ge2mg,
$$

$$
E(t_f)\ge E_{\rm reserve},
$$

$$
\sigma_{\rm arm}\le\sigma_{\rm allowable},
$$

$$
\Omega_i\le\Omega_{\max},
$$

$$
d_{\rm human}\ge d_{\rm safe},
$$

and

$$
e_{\rm tracking}\le e_{\max}.
$$

Now the computer isn't merely answering **“will my design fly?”**

It's searching:

$$
\boxed{
\text{What is the best flying screen permitted by physics?}
}
$$

And we can do something particularly useful: sweep the design space and generate a **feasibility map**.

For example, put screen payload on one axis and desired endurance on the other:

$$
(t_f,m_{\rm screen}).
$$

For every point, run the solver.

Then classify:

$$
\text{feasible}
\quad\text{or}\quad
\text{infeasible}.
$$

We'd obtain a boundary approximately like

$$
m_{\rm screen,max}(t_f).
$$

Our analytical theory predicts, for the fixed-disk-area model,

$$
m_{\max}\propto\frac1{t_f^2}.
$$

The numerical model would tell us what happens once we include **real motors, real propellers, screen drag, wind, maneuvering, structure, controller activity, battery discharge and rotor-size changes**.

That comparison between analytical theory and computational simulation is exactly how I'd approach this as an engineering research project.

I think the first program should therefore be deliberately small: **a Python feasibility solver for \(m\), \(m_b\), rotor diameter and endurance**, reproducing the closed-form \(m^{3/2}\) theory. Once that works, we turn it into the full nonlinear flying-screen simulator.
