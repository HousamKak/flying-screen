Yes. The right way to study this is as a **coupled fixed-point sizing problem**. The screen itself is not the difficult part; the question is whether the added propulsion and battery mass needed to carry the previous mass converges to a finite design.

### 1. Start with the total mass equation

Let

$$
m=m_s+m_f+m_c+m_p+m_b
$$

where:

* \(m_s\) = screen mass
* \(m_f\) = frame/gimbal/protection
* \(m_c\) = computer, sensors, tracking electronics
* \(m_p\) = motors + props + ESCs
* \(m_b\) = battery

Define everything that doesn't depend strongly on aircraft size as

$$
m_0=m_s+m_f+m_c
$$

so

$$
\boxed{m=m_0+m_p(m)+m_b(m)}
$$

That's the core equation.

Your concern is exactly that \(m_p\) and \(m_b\) are functions of \(m\).

---

## 2. Required thrust

At stationary hover:

$$
T_{\text{hover}}=mg
$$

For \(N\) rotors:

$$
\boxed{T_{\text{rotor,hover}}=\frac{mg}{N}}
$$

But you absolutely don't design the motors to max out at hover. Define a thrust margin \(\lambda\):

$$
T_{\max,\text{total}}=\lambda mg
$$

and therefore

$$
\boxed{T_{\max,\text{motor}}=
\frac{\lambda mg}{N}}
$$

For example, \(\lambda=2\) means the aircraft hovers around 50% of its maximum total thrust.

This equation feeds into motor selection.

---

# 3. Motor-mass coupling

Suppose the complete propulsion system has a maximum static specific thrust

$$
S_T=\frac{T_{\max}}{m_p}
$$

in N/kg.

Then:

$$
m_p=\frac{T_{\max,\text{total}}}{S_T}
$$

and because

$$
T_{\max,\text{total}}=\lambda mg
$$

we get

$$
\boxed{
m_p=\frac{\lambda g}{S_T}m
}
$$

Define

$$
f_p=\frac{\lambda g}{S_T}
$$

Then simply:

$$
\boxed{m_p=f_p m}
$$

This is interesting because the motor coupling itself is **not necessarily runaway**. It just consumes a fraction of your mass budget.

If, hypothetically,

$$
f_p=0.20
$$

then propulsion hardware ends up being about 20% of total aircraft mass.

The impossible condition is:

$$
f_p\geq1
$$

because then every kilogram requires at least another kilogram of propulsion hardware.

Realistically, battery/endurance is more likely to become the limiting feedback than the motors themselves.

---

# 4. The important rotor-power equation

This is where things get interesting.

For total rotor disk area \(A_d\),

$$
A_d=N\pi R^2
$$

where \(R\) is rotor radius.

Ideal induced velocity at hover:

$$
v_i=\sqrt{\frac{T}{2\rho A_d}}
$$

Since

$$
T=mg
$$

$$
v_i=\sqrt{\frac{mg}{2\rho A_d}}
$$

Ideal hover power:

$$
P_i=T v_i
$$

therefore

$$
\boxed{
P_i=
\frac{(mg)^{3/2}}
{\sqrt{2\rho A_d}}
}
$$

This equation is extremely important for your concept.

For **fixed rotor area**:

$$
\boxed{P\propto m^{3/2}}
$$

So doubling mass requires roughly

$$
2^{3/2}=2.83
$$

times the induced hover power.

This is the nonlinear coupling you were worried about.

---

# 5. Actual electrical power

Real rotors aren't ideal.

Let:

* \(FM\) = rotor figure of merit
* \(\eta_m\) = motor efficiency
* \(\eta_e\) = ESC/electrical efficiency

Then approximately:

$$
P_{\text{prop}}
=
\frac{P_i}
{FM\eta_m\eta_e}
+P_{\text{profile}}
$$

For a preliminary model we can combine efficiencies into one \(\eta\):

$$
\boxed{
P_{\text{prop}}
\approx
\frac{(mg)^{3/2}}
{\eta\sqrt{2\rho A_d}}
}
$$

Then your flying-screen electrical demand is

$$
\boxed{
P_{\text{total}}
=
P_{\text{prop}}
+
P_{\text{screen}}
+
P_{\text{computer}}
+
P_{\text{sensors}}
}
$$

Don't forget this part: unlike an ordinary drone, you're flying something that is itself continuously consuming electrical power.

---

# 6. Battery mass

Let battery pack specific energy be

$$
e_b \quad [Wh/kg]
$$

and required flight duration be

$$
t \quad [h]
$$

If only fraction \(u\) of the battery energy is usable because you want reserve/margin:

$$
\boxed{
m_b=
\frac{P_{\text{total}}t}
{e_bu}
}
$$

Now substitute the hover equation:

$$
m_b=
\frac{t}{e_bu}
\left[
\frac{(mg)^{3/2}}
{\eta\sqrt{2\rho A_d}}
+
P_{\text{screen}}
+
P_{\text{electronics}}
\right]
$$

And now you can see the loop.

---

# 7. Your complete coupled mass equation

Put everything together:

$$
m
=
m_0
+
f_pm
+
\frac{t}{e_bu}
\left[
\frac{(mg)^{3/2}}
{\eta\sqrt{2\rho A_d}}
+
P_0
\right]
$$

where

$$
P_0=P_{\text{screen}}+P_{\text{electronics}}
$$

Therefore:

$$
\boxed{
m=
m_0+
f_pm+
km^{3/2}
+
m_{b,0}
}
$$

with

$$
k=
\frac{t g^{3/2}}
{e_bu\eta\sqrt{2\rho A_d}}
$$

and

$$
m_{b,0}=
\frac{P_0t}{e_bu}
$$

This is essentially the **master sizing equation for the first version of your flying-screen idea**.

---

## 8. Why rotor size dramatically changes everything

Define disk loading:

$$
DL=\frac{mg}{A_d}
$$

Then hover induced velocity becomes

$$
v_i=\sqrt{\frac{DL}{2\rho}}
$$

and ideal power:

$$
P_i=mg\sqrt{\frac{DL}{2\rho}}
$$

So:

$$
\boxed{
\frac{P_i}{m}
=
g\sqrt{\frac{DL}{2\rho}}
}
$$

This tells us something very useful.

If you increase rotor area as the aircraft becomes heavier so that **disk loading stays constant**, then:

$$
P\propto m
$$

rather than

$$
P\propto m^{3/2}
$$

That's a huge difference.

So the engineering solution to the mass spiral isn't merely:

> bigger motors.

It's largely:

> **more rotor disk area.**

Large, slowly rotating rotors are much more attractive for something that needs to stay airborne beside a human for a long time.

---

# 9. In the constant-disk-loading case, the mass equation becomes beautiful

If

$$
P=c_Pm
$$

then battery mass is

$$
m_b=
\frac{c_Pt}{e_bu}m
$$

Define the battery mass fraction

$$
f_b=
\frac{c_Pt}{e_bu}
$$

Then

$$
m=m_0+f_pm+f_bm
$$

so:

$$
m(1-f_p-f_b)=m_0
$$

and finally

$$
\boxed{
m=
\frac{m_0}
{1-f_p-f_b}
}
$$

That equation answers your fundamental concern.

A finite flying machine exists when

$$
\boxed{
f_p+f_b<1
}
$$

As that sum approaches 1, mass explodes.

For example:

$$
f_p=0.20
$$

and

$$
f_b=0.30
$$

gives

$$
m=\frac{m_0}{1-0.2-0.3}
=2m_0
$$

So a 3 kg screen/frame/electronics package becomes about a 6 kg complete aircraft.

But if

$$
f_p+f_b=0.9
$$

then

$$
m=10m_0
$$

and the concept rapidly becomes absurd.

---

# 10. Following you introduces horizontal force

Suppose you're walking/running at velocity \(v\).

The screen creates aerodynamic drag:

$$
\boxed{
D=
\frac12\rho C_DA_fv^2
}
$$

where:

* \(C_D\) = drag coefficient
* \(A_f\) = frontal area of screen
* \(v\) = airspeed

If the vehicle also has to accelerate to follow you:

$$
F_x=ma_x+D
$$

Vertical thrust remains approximately

$$
F_z=m(g+a_z)
$$

Therefore total required thrust is

$$
\boxed{
T=
\sqrt{
[m(g+a_z)]^2+
[ma_x+D]^2
}
}
$$

For constant altitude:

$$
\boxed{
T=
\sqrt{
(mg)^2+
(ma_x+D)^2
}
}
$$

The aircraft tilt angle becomes

$$
\boxed{
\theta=
\tan^{-1}
\left(
\frac{ma_x+D}{mg}
\right)
}
$$

And this reveals another design problem for your idea:

**the aircraft needs to tilt to follow you, but you don't want the screen itself tilting.**

So mechanically I'd strongly consider:

$$
\text{flying platform}
\rightarrow
\text{2-axis stabilized screen gimbal}
$$

rather than rigidly attaching the display.

---

# 11. Screen/gimbal torque

The large flat screen will behave like a sail.

Wind force:

$$
D=\frac12\rho C_DA_fv^2
$$

If its aerodynamic center is distance \(r_{cp}\) from the gimbal axis:

$$
\boxed{
\tau_{\text{wind}}=D r_{cp}
}
$$

If the screen's center of gravity is offset from the axis by \(r_{cg}\):

$$
\tau_g=m_sgr_{cg}\sin\theta
$$

And acceleration requires:

$$
\tau_I=I\alpha
$$

Therefore the gimbal needs approximately:

$$
\boxed{
\tau_{\text{gimbal,max}}
>
D r_{cp}
+
m_sgr_{cg}\sin\theta
+
I\alpha
}
$$

Balancing the screen's CG directly through the gimbal axis would therefore save you a lot of continuous torque and electrical power.

# 12. The best way to actually calculate your prototype

I'd make the sizing calculation iterative.

Start with a guessed mass:

$$
m^{(0)}=m_0
$$

Then calculate:

$$
T^{(k)}=\lambda m^{(k)}g
$$

Select/estimate propulsion mass:

$$
m_p^{(k)}
=
m_p\left(T^{(k)}\right)
$$

Calculate hover power:

$$
P^{(k)}
=
\frac{
(m^{(k)}g)^{3/2}
}{
\eta\sqrt{2\rho A_d}
}
+P_0
$$

Battery:

$$
m_b^{(k)}
=
\frac{P^{(k)}t}{e_bu}
$$

Then update:

$$
\boxed{
m^{(k+1)}
=
m_0+
m_p^{(k)}
+
m_b^{(k)}
}
$$

Repeat until

$$
|m^{(k+1)}-m^{(k)}|<\epsilon
$$

If it settles to, say,

$$
5.6,\;6.1,\;6.25,\;6.29,\;6.30...
$$

the design converges.

If it goes

$$
5,\;7,\;11,\;20,\;50,\ldots
$$

you've mathematically demonstrated the mass spiral.

An even stronger local convergence criterion is

$$
\boxed{
\left|
\frac{d}{dm}
\left[m_p(m)+m_b(m)\right]
\right|<1
}
$$

at the design point.

That's basically the mathematical expression of the exact fear you described.

Exactly on the stabilization point: once the machine exists, keeping it airborne and following you is fundamentally a **nonlinear control/dynamics problem**.

But there is one important distinction: the “mass coupling” we were discussing is generally **not a physical differential equation in time**. It is a sizing constraint. For a battery-powered vehicle, its mass is essentially constant while flying:

$$
\boxed{\dot m = 0}
$$

The battery loses energy, not appreciable mass. Even from \(E=mc^2\),

$$
\dot m=-\frac{P}{c^2}
$$

so at \(P=1000\rm\,W\),

$$
\dot m\approx-1.1\times10^{-14}\rm\,kg/s
$$

which we can completely ignore.

So I would separate your flying-screen model into **two coupled systems**.

### 1. The design/sizing coupling

Before building it,

$$
\boxed{
m=m_0+m_{\rm motor}(m)+m_{\rm battery}(m)
}
$$

For example,

$$
m_b=
\frac{t_fP(m)}{e_b\eta_b}
$$

and approximately

$$
P(m)=
\frac{(mg)^{3/2}}
{\eta_p\sqrt{2\rho A}}
+P_e
$$

so

$$
\boxed{
m=
m_0+
m_p(m)
+
\frac{t_f}{e_b\eta_b}
\left[
\frac{(mg)^{3/2}}
{\eta_p\sqrt{2\rho A}}
+P_e
\right]
}
$$

That's an **implicit algebraic equation**, not an ODE.

If you really want to study whether the mass feedback converges using differential-equation language, you can introduce an artificial sizing variable \(\tau\):

$$
\boxed{
\frac{dm}{d\tau}
=
m_0+m_p(m)+m_b(m)-m
}
$$

The equilibrium is

$$
\frac{dm}{d\tau}=0
$$

which gives exactly the real sizing equation.

This is useful because stability of the sizing equilibrium requires

$$
\frac{d}{dm}
[m_p(m)+m_b(m)]<1.
$$

That's mathematically a very nice way of representing your original “bigger mass → bigger motors → bigger mass” concern.

---

### 2. The actual flight differential equations

Once you've built the thing, \(m\) is fixed and the real coupled ODEs begin.

For translation:

$$
\boxed{\dot{\mathbf r}=\mathbf v}
$$

$$
\boxed{
m\dot{\mathbf v}
=
\mathbf R(\mathbf q)
\begin{bmatrix}
0\\
0\\
T
\end{bmatrix}
-mg\mathbf e_z
+\mathbf F_D
+\mathbf F_{\rm disturbance}
}
$$

where

$$
\mathbf r=
\begin{bmatrix}
x\\y\\z
\end{bmatrix},
\qquad
\mathbf v=
\begin{bmatrix}
v_x\\v_y\\v_z
\end{bmatrix}.
$$

Aerodynamic drag can initially be modeled as

$$
\boxed{
\mathbf F_D
=
-\frac12\rho C_D A
|\mathbf v_{\rm rel}|\mathbf v_{\rm rel}
}
$$

and

$$
\mathbf v_{\rm rel}
=
\mathbf v-\mathbf v_{\rm air}.
$$

So already this is nonlinear.

---

### 3. Rotational dynamics

The rigid-body attitude satisfies Euler's equation:

$$
\boxed{
\mathbf J\dot{\boldsymbol\omega}
+
\boldsymbol\omega\times
(\mathbf J\boldsymbol\omega)
=
\boldsymbol\tau
}
$$

or

$$
\boxed{
\dot{\boldsymbol\omega}
=
\mathbf J^{-1}
\left[
\boldsymbol\tau
-
\boldsymbol\omega\times
(\mathbf J\boldsymbol\omega)
\right]
}
$$

with

$$
\boldsymbol\omega=
\begin{bmatrix}
p\\q\\r
\end{bmatrix}.
$$

The attitude itself can be represented using a quaternion:

$$
\boxed{
\dot{\mathbf q}
=
\frac12
\Omega(\boldsymbol\omega)\mathbf q
}
$$

rather than Euler angles, which avoids the singularities you'd get with roll-pitch-yaw.

So your basic aircraft states are already

$$
\boxed{
\mathbf x=
[
x,y,z,
v_x,v_y,v_z,
q_0,q_1,q_2,q_3,
p,q,r
]^T
}
$$

That's 13 states.

---

### 4. The motors introduce another set of differential equations

Motor thrust isn't instantaneous.

For motor \(i\), a simple first-order model is

$$
\boxed{
\tau_m\dot\Omega_i
=
\Omega_{i,\rm cmd}-\Omega_i
}
$$

or

$$
\dot\Omega_i
=
\frac{\Omega_{i,\rm cmd}-\Omega_i}{\tau_m}.
$$

Rotor thrust:

$$
\boxed{T_i=k_T\Omega_i^2}
$$

Rotor reaction torque:

$$
\boxed{Q_i=k_Q\Omega_i^2}
$$

Total thrust:

$$
\boxed{
T=\sum_{i=1}^N k_T\Omega_i^2
}
$$

and the rotor positions produce roll/pitch torque:

$$
\boxed{
\boldsymbol\tau
=
\sum_i
\mathbf r_i\times
\begin{bmatrix}
0\\0\\T_i
\end{bmatrix}
+
\sum_i
\begin{bmatrix}
0\\0\\s_iQ_i
\end{bmatrix}
}
$$

where \(s_i=\pm1\) depending on propeller rotation direction.

Now your controller doesn't directly command force. It commands motor speeds, which produce force, which accelerates the mass.

So the causal chain is

$$
u_i
\rightarrow
\Omega_i
\rightarrow
T_i
\rightarrow
(T,\tau)
\rightarrow
(\dot v,\dot\omega)
\rightarrow
(v,\omega)
\rightarrow
(r,q).
$$

That's the real control-system coupling.

---

### 5. And battery energy is another differential equation

Instead of mass changing, introduce battery energy \(E\):

$$
\boxed{
\dot E=-P
}
$$

with

$$
P=P_{\rm motors}+P_{\rm screen}+P_{\rm computing}.
$$

Or normalized state of charge \(s\):

$$
s=\frac{E}{E_{\max}}
$$

so

$$
\boxed{
\dot s=
-\frac{P}{E_{\max}}
}
$$

with \(E_{\max}\) expressed in joules.

Motor power approximately depends on rotor speed as

$$
P_i\propto\Omega_i^3,
$$

so

$$
\boxed{
P_{\rm propulsion}
\approx
\sum_i k_P\Omega_i^3.
}
$$

Therefore

$$
\boxed{
\dot s=
-\frac{
\sum_i k_P\Omega_i^3+P_{\rm screen}+P_{\rm computer}
}{
E_{\max}
}
}
$$

which means the control system affects battery depletion.

That's a genuine dynamic coupling.

## So the complete model is something like

$$
\boxed{
\begin{aligned}
\dot{\mathbf r}&=\mathbf v \\[2mm]
m\dot{\mathbf v}&=
R(q)\mathbf T-mg\mathbf e_z+\mathbf F_D \\[2mm]
J\dot{\omega}&=
\tau-\omega\times J\omega \\[2mm]
\dot q&=\frac12\Omega(\omega)q \\[2mm]
\dot\Omega_i&=
\frac{\Omega_{i,c}-\Omega_i}{\tau_m} \\[2mm]
\dot s&=
-\frac{P(\Omega_1,\ldots,\Omega_N)}{E_{\max}} \\[2mm]
\dot m&=0
\end{aligned}
}
$$

while outside this dynamic system you have the design constraint

$$
\boxed{
0=
m-m_0-m_p(m)-m_b(m)
}
$$

So mathematically the complete engineering model is actually closer to a **differential-algebraic system (DAE)** than “a couple of ODEs”:

$$
\boxed{
\dot x=f(x,u,p)
}
$$

together with

$$
\boxed{
0=g(x,u,p).
}
$$

Here \(p\) contains design parameters such as

$$
m,\;J,\;A_{\rm rotor},\;E_{\max},\;k_T,\;k_Q,\ldots
$$

and those parameters themselves have to satisfy the mass/thrust/battery sizing equations.

That distinction is useful: **mass feedback determines whether the machine can exist; the Newton–Euler ODEs determine whether it can fly stably; the controller determines whether it can follow you without falling over.**

The next really interesting step is to linearize these nonlinear equations around hover, because then we get the actual state-space model

$$
\boxed{\dot x=Ax+Bu,\qquad y=Cx+Du}
$$

and we can see exactly which modes need PID/LQR control and whether the flying screen is controllable.

\Omega_{i,\rm cmd}-\Omega_i
}
$$

or

$$
\dot\Omega_i
=
\frac{\Omega_{i,\rm cmd}-\Omega_i}{\tau_m}.
$$

Rotor thrust:

$$
\boxed{T_i=k_T\Omega_i^2}
$$

Rotor reaction torque:

$$
\boxed{Q_i=k_Q\Omega_i^2}
$$

Total thrust:

$$
\boxed{
T=\sum_{i=1}^N k_T\Omega_i^2
}
$$

and the rotor positions produce roll/pitch torque:

$$
\boxed{
\boldsymbol\tau
=
\sum_i
\mathbf r_i\times
\begin{bmatrix}
0\\0\\T_i
\end{bmatrix}
+
\sum_i
\begin{bmatrix}
0\\0\\s_iQ_i
\end{bmatrix}
}
$$

where \(s_i=\pm1\) depending on propeller rotation direction.

Now your controller doesn't directly command force. It commands motor speeds, which produce force, which accelerates the mass.

So the causal chain is

$$
u_i
\rightarrow
\Omega_i
\rightarrow
T_i
\rightarrow
(T,\tau)
\rightarrow
(\dot v,\dot\omega)
\rightarrow
(v,\omega)
\rightarrow
(r,q).
$$

That's the real control-system coupling.

### 5. And battery energy is another differential equation

Instead of mass changing, introduce battery energy \(E\):

$$
\boxed{
\dot E=-P
}
$$

with

$$
P=P_{\rm motors}+P_{\rm screen}+P_{\rm computing}.
$$

Or normalized state of charge \(s\):

$$
s=\frac{E}{E_{\max}}
$$

so

$$
\boxed{
\dot s=
-\frac{P}{E_{\max}}
}
$$

with \(E_{\max}\) expressed in joules.

Motor power approximately depends on rotor speed as

$$
P_i\propto\Omega_i^3,
$$

so

$$
\boxed{
P_{\rm propulsion}
\approx
\sum_i k_P\Omega_i^3.
}
$$

Therefore

$$
\boxed{
\dot s=
-\frac{
\sum_i k_P\Omega_i^3+P_{\rm screen}+P_{\rm computer}
}{
E_{\max}
}
}
$$

which means the control system affects battery depletion.

That's a genuine dynamic coupling.

#### Why the lumped `ST` model is wrong for your case

Lumping motors and battery into one `mp` hides that they scale off different requirements:

- **Motor mass** scales with peak **thrust** (a burst quantity).
- **Battery mass** scales with hover **power times time** (an endurance quantity).

You want a screen to hover next to you for a work session. That makes endurance the driving requirement, so the battery must be split out.

#### The full equation set for your setup

**Mass budget**

```math
m=mpay+mstr+mmot+mbat
```

**Structure**, taken as a fraction of gross mass (frame, arms, mounts, gimbal):

```math
mstr=fs m,fs≈0.15–0.25
```

**Motors + ESCs + props**, sized by the peak thrust requirement `Tmax⁡=λmg`, with `Sm` the motor-only specific thrust (100 to 150 N/kg is realistic today):

```math
mmot=λgSm m
```

**Hover power**, momentum theory over total disk area `A=NπD2/4`, with FM the figure of merit (0.5 to 0.7) and `η` the electrical efficiency (motor times ESC, \~0.75):


```math
mbat=Pelec teb
```

Substitute everything back into the mass budget and group the terms:

```math
α m−β m3/2=mpay
```

```math
α=1−fs−λgSm,β=t g3/2η eb FM2ρA
```

`α` is your **thrust coupling** (the thing you were worried about). `β` is your **energy coupling**. Note that `α` is just a number less than 1 and it only has to stay positive, which is easy. `β` carries the `m3/2`, and that is where the design dies.

The left side is not monotonic. It peaks and then falls, so there is a **hard maximum payload** no amount of engineering can exceed at a given endurance. Setting `d/dm=0`:

```math
m∗=4α29β2, mpaymax⁡=4α327β2 
```

Beyond that, no solution exists. Not "heavy", not "expensive". Nonexistent.

#### The scaling laws that decide your project

Substituting `β` into the box:

```math
mpaymax⁡  ∝  α3 η2eb2 FM2 ρ At2
```

Read that carefully:

- **Payload capacity falls as ********`1/t2`********.** Want 4x the flight time? You get 1/16th the payload.
- Battery energy density enters **squared**. That is why custom cells feel so tempting and disappoint so hard: a 20% better cell buys 44% more payload, one time.
- Disk area enters **linearly**, so it is your best lever. Diameter enters as `D2`.

### But there's one very important qualification

The brutal

$$

$$

Because

$$

$$

$$

$$

then

$$
A\propto m.
$$

Substituting that into the hover-power equation,

P\propto\frac{m^{3/2}}{\sqrt m}

$$
\boxed{P\propto m.}

$$
m_b\propto m.

The \(m^{3/2}\) catastrophe disappears.

> the exponents make an untethered flying screen fundamentally impossible.

I would say:

> **fixed rotor area + onboard battery + long endurance creates a nonlinear feasibility wall.**

### The phrase I would change most

> "The exponents are what kill you, not the constants."

Mathematically, that's only partly true.

The exponent determines the **asymptotic behavior**.

The constants determine **where you hit the wall**.

For example,

$$
m_{\text{pay,max}}
=
\frac{4\alpha^3}{27\beta^2}
$$

and

$$
\beta\propto
\frac{t}
{\eta e_bFM\sqrt A}.
$$

So doubling disk area, increasing energy density, changing efficiency, etc. can shift the critical point substantially.

For engineering at finite scales, constants matter enormously.

The better statement is:

$$
\boxed{
\text{The exponent creates the wall; the constants determine where the wall is.}
}
$$

### And there's an even deeper result

Write the design equation as

$$
F(m)=
\alpha m-\beta m^{3/2}-m_{\rm pay}=0.
$$

There are three regimes.

For

$$
m_{\rm pay}<m_{\rm pay,max},
$$

there are generally **two positive mathematical solutions**.

At

$$
m_{\rm pay}=m_{\rm pay,max},
$$

there is one repeated solution:

$$
F(m^*)=0,
\qquad
F'(m^*)=0.
$$

Above it,

$$
m_{\rm pay}>m_{\rm pay,max},
$$

there is

$$
\boxed{\text{no solution}.}
$$

Graphically it's this shape:

$$
m_{\rm pay}
$$

rises with aircraft mass, reaches a peak, and then actually decreases because battery mass starts consuming more gross mass than the additional gross mass provides.

That is a genuinely beautiful result.
